For CPG companies, the real payoff of agentic AI is growth, not cost — and the distinction is operational, not rhetorical. McKinsey's October 2026 analysis of agentic AI in consumer packaged goods argues exactly this: FMCG companies have concentrated AI on cost programs and left revenue unrealized, while the payoff sits across commercial operations — planning, promotions, pricing, retail execution — where agentic systems can finally close the loop between analysis and action.[[1]](#ref-mck-2026-cpg) Independent European retail coverage of the same analysis reached the same conclusion: the sector "leaves revenue on the table" by aiming AI at savings rather than growth.[[2]](#ref-retaildetail-2026-cpg)

The reason agents change this specific equation is structural. Commercial operations in FMCG have always been data-rich and action-poor: the analysis exists (syndicated panels, retailer POS, promotion histories), but the actions it implies — reprice this SKU in this channel, reallocate this trade spend, fix this distribution gap before the weekend — die in handoffs between analysts, account managers and retailer processes. Agents are the first technology that acts on the analysis under supervision. The value was never in better reports; it was in the loop the reports never closed.

## Executive Summary

- The CPG payoff is growth: agentic AI applied across commercial operations — planning, promotions, pricing, retail execution — rather than concentrated in cost programs.[[1]](#ref-mck-2026-cpg)
- Trade promotion is the canonical target: among the largest discretionary spends in CPG (historically ~15-20%+ of gross revenue), long managed with weak measurement — the classic 2013 Strategy& baseline found most promotions failing to break even and post-event analysis rare; discipline is the opportunity, and the data is dated, so treat it as the historical baseline it is.[[3]](#ref-strategyand-trade)
- Agents work the commercial cycle end to end: demand planning, promotion design and settlement, price execution, retail execution monitoring — each with defined autonomy and human approval gates where money moves.[[4]](#ref-bcg-2026-formula)
- The growth payoff is claimed honestly: baseline first (the value-ledger discipline), then booked revenue against it — not dashboard optimism.[[4]](#ref-bcg-2026-formula)
- The operating model matters more than any single use case: commercial missions with named P&L owners, evaluation on retailer-specific distributions, and the governance that lets agents act without unpriced risk.

## Why cost-only AI leaves revenue on the table

The sector's AI history explains the bias. Cost programs — supply chain optimization, back-office automation, forecasting infrastructure — are measurable, contained, and owned by functions with engineering budgets. They were the right first targets, and they worked: forecast accuracy improved, cost-to-serve fell. But they optimized the *given* commercial plan. The revenue implications of a better plan — pricing that responds to elasticity, promotions that earn their spend, distribution that closes gaps before they age — sat in commercial teams whose analytics were descriptive and whose actions were manual.[[1]](#ref-mck-2026-cpg)

The asymmetry compounds. Every quarter, a cost-only program leaves three revenue leaks running: promotion spend that did not earn its discount (settled on shipment, never on performance), price gaps versus channel and competitor that persist for weeks, and distribution voids that age from days into quarters. Each leak is individually modest and collectively the difference between flat and growing revenue in a low-growth category. RetailDetail's coverage of the McKinsey analysis captures the industry framing: the money left on the table is a choice, not a mystery.[[2]](#ref-retaildetail-2026-cpg)

## The trade-spend baseline — dated, and still damning

The anchor number deserves its own section because it frames the promotion problem that agents address. Strategy&'s cross-client analysis (2013 — dated, and current market averages differ; the point is the discipline gap it documented, not a current benchmark) found trade promotion among the largest discretionary spends in CPG at commonly ~15-20%+ of gross revenue, with a large share of promotions historically failing to break even and post-event analysis rare across the industry.[[3]](#ref-strategyand-trade)

A decade of RGM software improved the analytics. What persisted is the execution gap: the post-event analysis existed, but the next event's design, allocation and settlement still ran through manual processes and retailer timelines, so the lessons arrived late and applied partially. That is precisely the gap agentic systems close — which is why the promotion cycle is the canonical first mission for commercial agents, developed as the worked [revenue-recovery analysis](/en/insights/recovering-revenue-inventory-pricing-promotions) in this publication.

| Stage | Agent work | Autonomy band | Human gate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Demand planning | Forecast per SKU-channel-week; flag demand signals; propose plan adjustments | Proposes; planner approves material changes | Weekly planning review |
| Promotion design | Event scenarios with elasticity and funding constraints; settlement rules drafted | Drafts scenarios | Account manager + RGM approve design |
| Promotion settlement | Validate against sell-through; flag underperformance mid-event; draft claims | Monitors and drafts; escalates anomalies | RGM signs settlement |
| Pricing | Channel price monitoring; gap detection; repricing proposals within guardrails | Proposes within guardrails; executes trivial | Pricing lead approves material moves |
| Retail execution | Shelf/distribution monitoring from image and retailer data; prioritized action lists by store | Monitors; drafts actions | Field/retail team executes |

The autonomy bands follow the [supervision matrix](/en/insights/human-in-the-loop-vs-human-on-the-loop): money-moving actions (settlement, material repricing) get approval gates; monitoring and drafting run autonomously with sampled review. The stages share infrastructure — the golden sets are retailer-specific (Walmart's data discipline is not Carrefour's), the evaluation harness per the [evaluation framework](/en/insights/ai-agent-evaluation-for-business-outcomes), and the audit trails per the [governance stack](/en/insights/agentic-ai-governance-by-design) — because an agent repricing SKUs in a retailer relationship is exactly the blast-radius case governance exists for.

Two stages deserve the emphasis. **Pricing agents** are where growth compounds: elasticity-aware proposals, competitor and channel gaps detected in days not weeks, guardrails encoding the commercial policy ("never below X in this channel," "max Y% move per cycle"). The guardrail design is the product — it encodes what the commercial team would approve anyway, and lets the agent act at the speed the market moves. **Retail execution agents** close the last mile: distribution voids and shelf issues detected from retailer and image data, prioritized by revenue at stake, pushed to field teams as specific store-level actions. The revenue there is unglamorous and real — voids that age are the cheapest growth a commercial team will ever recover.

## Claiming the growth honestly

The growth playbook's credibility rests on the measurement discipline this publication has built case by case: baseline before rollout, holdout where feasible, booked value with confidence grades in the [value ledger](/en/insights/the-ai-transformation-value-ledger), and the [evidence-quality ladder](/en/insights/measuring-ai-value-beyond-productivity) governing what gets claimed in the boardroom. BCG's agentic-AI value research — with its ~3x multiplier for systematically controlled deployments, per the validant.ai analysis — is the general finding; the commercial version is specific: agents whose value you can reconstruct (which events, which prices, which voids, against which baseline) are agents whose budget survives the next planning cycle.[[4]](#ref-bcg-2026-formula)

Practically, the commercial missions open ledger entries like any other: baseline promotion performance by category, price-gap persistence, void-aging curves; mission outcomes booked as incremental revenue or protected margin against those baselines, with the attribution method stated (matched test-control markets where the retailer relationship allows; before/after with trend adjustment where it does not). McKinsey's growth-payoff argument becomes an operating claim precisely when it is booked this way — and not before.[[1]](#ref-mck-2026-cpg)

## The operating model: commercial missions, not tool rollouts

The transformation pattern this publication has developed in the abstract lands concretely in FMCG commercial organizations. The unit is the commercial mission — "recover X points of promotion ROI in category Y by Q2," owned by a commercial lead with decision rights, staffed with agent engineers and RGM analysts, run on retailer-specific golden sets, exit criteria and harvest into a capability library that makes the next category cheaper, per the [mission factory](/en/insights/the-agentic-mission-factory). The portfolio rule holds: three to five concurrent commercial missions, per the [prioritization method](/en/insights/prioritizing-high-value-ai-missions), starting where the arithmetic is largest — which, in most CPG portfolios, is promotions and pricing before shelf analytics.

The organizational changes follow: commercial planners and account managers become supervisors of agent output (the role shift analyzed in the [team-design article](/en/insights/designing-human-and-ai-product-teams)), pricing policy gets encoded as guardrails (a governance artifact, not a slide), and the [90-day rhythm](/en/insights/the-90-day-ai-transformation-roadmap) governs the first cycle. The cost side does not stop — supply-chain agents get their own mission in the [decision-intelligence analysis](/en/insights/ai-native-supply-chain-decision-intelligence) — but the growth side finally gets funded at the level the opportunity justifies.

## Key Takeaways

- The agentic-AI payoff in CPG is growth: McKinsey's October 2026 analysis and independent retail coverage converge on revenue left on the table by cost-only AI programs.[[1]](#ref-mck-2026-cpg)[[2]](#ref-retaildetail-2026-cpg)
- Trade promotion is the canonical first mission — largest discretionary spend, weakest historical measurement discipline; the 2013 Strategy& baseline documents the gap, dated as it is, and the agent opportunity is closing the execution loop, not better analytics.[[3]](#ref-strategyand-trade)
- Agents work the whole commercial cycle: planning, promotion design and settlement, pricing with guardrails, retail execution — autonomy bands by task, human gates where money moves.
- Growth is claimed with the same ledger discipline as cost: baselines, holdouts, confidence-graded bookings — the mechanism that makes the growth argument survive a CFO.[[4]](#ref-bcg-2026-formula)
- The operating model is commercial missions with P&L owners, retailer-specific evaluation, and a capability library — not tool rollouts into account teams.

## What Leaders Should Do Next

1. **Split the AI portfolio's value at stake by cost versus revenue.** (CFO office + commercial lead, first 30 days.) The exercise takes a week and produces the strategic number: what fraction of the opportunity the current program is structurally ignoring.[[1]](#ref-mck-2026-cpg)
2. **Baseline the three revenue leaks.** (RGM + finance, first 30 days.) Promotion ROI by category, price-gap persistence by channel, void-aging curves — before any agent ships; these are the ledger baselines everything later books against.
3. **Stand up the first commercial mission: promotion cycle, one category, one quarter.** (Commercial lead, days 30-90.) Settlement validation and mid-event flagging first (approval-gated), design assistance second; golden set from two years of event history per the evaluation discipline.
4. **Encode pricing policy as guardrails.** (Pricing lead + governance partner, first 60 days.) The rules the team applies anyway — floors, ceilings, move limits, channel constraints — written as execution-layer constraints the pricing agent operates within, with the audit trail behind them.
5. **Review the portfolio at the 90-day gate with the growth split on the page.** (Executive committee, day 90.) Cost missions and commercial missions, booked value side by side — and the next quarter's funding follows the arithmetic, per the portfolio discipline.[[4]](#ref-bcg-2026-formula)
